01

Three workbooks, one dashboard, zero untraceable numbers

DATA CONSOLIDATIONRECONCILIATIONSTAKEHOLDER REPORTING

The problem

A partner program's reporting lived in separate workbooks — one for certifications, one for client activation requests, one for subscription accounts. Answering a basic program question meant opening all three, and no two answers were guaranteed to agree.

What I did

Consolidated 3,000+ records into a four-tab analytics dashboard with a shared structure across views. Every metric drills down to the underlying records and exports to CSV, so any number on screen can be traced and taken away by whoever's asking.

The judgment call

Before shipping I reconciled the dashboard field by field against the source workbooks and dropped every figure the sources couldn't support. Trust was the actual feature: a metric nobody can trace gets ignored, and a wrong one gets remembered.

Outcome

Questions that used to require three files now have one answer in one place. It took 18 versions of stakeholder feedback and reconciliation passes before I called it done.

02

Finding where support burden actually comes from

TAXONOMY DESIGNOPERATIONS ANALYSISPERFORMANCE ENGINEERING

The problem

A support-reduction initiative had a goal but no starting line. Roughly 11,000 support tickets across 700+ vendor accounts existed only as unstructured titles in a queue. Nobody could say where the burden came from, so nobody could prove it was shrinking.

What I did

Derived a two-level classification taxonomy (12 categories, ~35 topics) from the free-text titles and applied it across the full dataset with zero records left uncategorized. That exposed the distribution for the first time: a small group of accounts drove nearly half of all volume, and self-service opportunities clustered in a handful of predictable categories.

The judgment call

The analysis shipped as a self-contained browser dashboard that started at 2.3 MB — too heavy to share. I rebuilt the data layer with dictionary encoding, column-oriented storage, and compression, cutting it to 466 KB, then verified full data parity myself across every record and field before anyone saw it.

Outcome

The initiative got measurable baselines for its key results, and leadership got a shareable view of where support effort actually goes instead of anecdotes about it.

~11Ktickets analyzed
0uncategorized records
4.9×smaller payload, full parity

Transfers toCustomer support and success analytics in any industry, SaaS onboarding, call-center and service-desk operations, ecommerce returns analysis, IT service management — every queue-based system has this same hidden concentration.

03

An internal tool for people who can't see their own data

UX & VISUALIZATIONALERT DESIGNTEAM OF 4 · 5 WEEKS

The problem

Analysts working inside a client's data had no easy way to see when data quality slipped, or where. Our four-person team entered the company's internal innovation challenge with a data-quality monitoring dashboard, and five weeks to make it real.

What I owned

Two of six workstreams: the interface and the alerting. The design rule for the interface was the first thirty seconds — opening it should tell you what's wrong, where, and how serious, before you click anything. The alerting side was about who gets told when a metric slips, and how to route that without drowning people in noise.

The judgment call

Access control shaped the design as much as usability: views masked by default, small groups suppressed to protect individuals, every data reveal written to a log. And a week before presenting, we cut every claim the shipped code didn't actually back — our refresh ran on a daily cycle, not in real time, and saying so plainly beat getting caught on it in Q&A.

Outcome

We presented to a senior leadership panel and took five minutes of questions harder than any of our dry runs, which is what we'd practiced for. Precise beat polished.
